What happens to particles when matter is heated or cooled?

The particle model says that all matter is made of tiny particles. The particles are always moving, and there are spaces between them. In a solid, particles are close together and vibrate in place. In a liquid, particles stay close but can slide past one another. In a gas, particles are far apart and move freely.

Heating transfers energy to matter. Its particles move faster. Cooling removes energy, so particles move more slowly. During a phase change, matter changes state: for example, a solid melts into a liquid or a liquid evaporates into a gas.

Remember: Particles themselves do not melt, disappear or get bigger. Their motion and arrangement change.

1 LEARN IT

Expansion and contraction

Most materials expand when heated and contract when cooled. In many solids, heating makes particles vibrate more and the average space between them increases. The particles themselves do not become larger.

A liquid thermometer uses this idea: as the liquid warms, it expands and rises in a narrow tube. Bridges and sidewalks are also designed to allow for changes in size as temperatures change.

Real-world connection: Expansion gaps help structures handle temperature changes without pushing against nearby sections.

1 LEARN IT

Water, volume and density

Water behaves differently from many other substances when it freezes. Ice takes up more space than the same mass of liquid water. This means ice is less dense than liquid water, so it floats.

Density describes how much mass is packed into a certain volume. If the same mass takes up more space, its density is lower. When ice melts, its mass stays the same even though its volume changes.

Real-world connection: Ice floating on a lake forms a surface layer. That layer helps insulate the water below during cold weather.
